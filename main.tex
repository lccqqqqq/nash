\begin{abstract}
We study the entanglement structure of Nash equilibria in multi-player quantum games
on ring geometries, where players' strategies are local unitaries acting on a shared fiducial state
represented as a matrix product state (MPS).
Using simultaneous differential best response dynamics with adaptive learning rate scheduling,
we numerically compute Nash equilibria for quantum Prisoner's Dilemma games with 3 and 6 players.
For diagonal (commuting) payoff Hamiltonians, the welfare-optimal Nash equilibrium consistently
resides on the GHZ orbit, and this robustness persists under the addition of non-commutative perturbations.
We observe that low bond dimension MPS ($\chi = 2$) suffice to capture the optimal equilibria,
suggesting an area-law entanglement structure for the best Nash equilibrium.
We discuss the role of exploitability thresholds and the topology of the Nash variety
in producing sudden welfare drops during optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Quantum game formulation}

We consider an $L$-player quantum game where each player $i$ controls a single qubit.
The joint state of all players is described by a quantum state $\ket{\psi} \in (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes L}$.
Each player's strategy consists of a single-qubit unitary $U_i \in \mathrm{SU}(2)$ applied to their qubit.
Given a strategy profile $(U_1, \ldots, U_L)$ applied to a fiducial state $\ket{\psi_0}$, the resulting state is
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi} = (U_1 \otimes U_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes U_L) \ket{\psi_0}.
\end{equation}

The payoff for player $i$ is defined as the expectation value of a Hermitian operator $H_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:payoff}
    E_i = \bra{\psi} H_i \ket{\psi}.
\end{equation}
In the quantum Prisoner's Dilemma, the Hamiltonians $H_i$ are diagonal in the computational basis,
\begin{equation}
    H_i = \sum_{\mathbf{s} \in \{0,1\}^L} h_i(\mathbf{s}) \ketbra{\mathbf{s}}{\mathbf{s}},
\end{equation}
where $h_i(\mathbf{s})$ assigns a real-valued payoff to each measurement outcome $\mathbf{s} = (s_1, \ldots, s_L)$.

\subsection{Payoff structure}

For $L = 3$ players, the payoff values are given in Table~\ref{tab:payoffs}.
The game is symmetric under cyclic permutation of the players:
\begin{equation}
    h_i(\mathbf{s}) = h_{i+1 \bmod 3}(\sigma(\mathbf{s})),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the corresponding cyclic permutation of the bit string.
Mutual cooperation ($\mathbf{s} = 000$) yields a payoff of 6 per player,
while mutual defection ($\mathbf{s} = 111$) yields only 2---the characteristic tension of the Prisoner's Dilemma.
A single defector receives 10 while the remaining cooperators receive 3 or 0,
creating an incentive to deviate from cooperation.

For general $L$, players are arranged on a ring with pairwise interactions.
Each player interacts with their two nearest neighbors via a 2-player Prisoner's Dilemma Hamiltonian
$H^{(2)}$ with payoffs $(R, S, T, P) = (3, 0, 5, 1)$,
and the total payoff for player $i$ is the average over incident edges:
\begin{equation}
    H_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( H^{(2)}_{i, i+1} + H^{(2)}_{i, i-1} \right),
\end{equation}
where indices are taken modulo $L$.

\subsection{Matrix product state representation}

For large $L$, storing the full state vector $\ket{\psi} \in (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes L}$ is intractable.
We represent quantum states as matrix product states (MPS) with open boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi} = \sum_{s_1, \ldots, s_L} A_1^{s_1} A_2^{s_2} \cdots A_L^{s_L} \ket{s_1 s_2 \cdots s_L},
\end{equation}
where each $A_j^{s_j}$ is a matrix of dimension $\chi_{j-1} \times \chi_j$ with $\chi_0 = \chi_L = 1$.
The bond dimension $\chi = \max_j \chi_j$ controls the entanglement capacity of the ansatz.
For $\chi = 1$, the state is a product state (zero entanglement);
for $\chi = 2^{\lfloor L/2 \rfloor}$, the MPS can represent any state exactly.

In practice, we work with bond dimension $\chi = 2$, which is sufficient to capture
states with moderate entanglement, including GHZ-type states
$\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}} = (\ket{0}^{\otimes L} + \ket{1}^{\otimes L})/\sqrt{2}$.

\subsection{Nash equilibrium and exploitability}

A state $\ket{\psi}$ constitutes an $\varepsilon$-Nash equilibrium if no player can increase their payoff
by more than $\varepsilon$ through a unilateral unitary deviation:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{U_i \in \mathrm{SU}(2)} \bra{\psi_i(U_i)} H_i \ket{\psi_i(U_i)} - \bra{\psi} H_i \ket{\psi} < \varepsilon \quad \forall\, i,
\end{equation}
where $\ket{\psi_i(U_i)}$ denotes the state with $U_i$ applied only to qubit $i$.
The left-hand side defines the \emph{exploitability} $\varepsilon_i(\psi)$ of player $i$.

We find Nash equilibria using \emph{simultaneous differential best response dynamics}.
At each iteration $n$, we compute the gradient of each player's payoff with respect to
their local unitary:
\begin{equation}
    M_i = \pdv{E_i}{U_i} = \tr_{j \neq i}\left[ H_i \ketbra{\psi}{\psi} \right],
\end{equation}
where the partial trace is over all qubits except $i$.
The unitary update is extracted via the SVD of the matrix $\mathbb{I} - \alpha M_i$:
if $\mathbb{I} - \alpha M_i = Y \Sigma Z^\dagger$, the update is $U_i = Z Y^\dagger$,
which is the unitary closest to the gradient step in operator norm.
All players update simultaneously, and the procedure is iterated until
the total exploitability $\sum_i \varepsilon_i < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$.

The exploitability $\varepsilon_i$ is computed by globally optimizing over $\mathrm{SU}(2)$
using differential evolution,
ensuring that the reported Nash equilibria are validated against arbitrary (not just infinitesimal) deviations.

\subsection{Algorithmic refinements}
\label{sec:algorithm-refinements}

Several algorithmic improvements were necessary to obtain reliable Nash equilibria
across a range of system sizes and Hamiltonian types.

\paragraph{Subroutine learning rate scheduling.}
When Nash equilibria are not found by the differential best response solver,
the failure is typically due to insufficient learning rates or premature termination,
rather than the algorithm converging to a non-Nash state.
We address this by implementing adaptive retry logic in the solver:
when convergence fails, the subroutine learning rate $\alpha$ is increased
and the solver is restarted. Analysis of failed runs confirms this diagnosis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At low learning rates ($\alpha = 0.01$), the exploitability decreases but does not
    reach the threshold within the allotted iterations---a premature termination artifact.
    \item At moderate learning rates ($\alpha \approx 0.09$), convergence improves significantly.
    \item If $\alpha$ exceeds a critical threshold ($\alpha \gtrsim 3$),
    the exploitability exhibits limit cycles and fails to settle,
    an artifact of overshooting in the best response dynamics.
    \item An intermediate regime ($\alpha \approx 2$) can produce states with
    constant payoff but nonzero exploitability---locally stable but globally unstable configurations.
\end{itemize}
Note that $\alpha$ does not directly set the step size; it scales the matrix
$\mathbb{I} - \alpha M_i$ whose SVD yields the unitary update,
so the effective step size also depends on the gradient magnitude.
These regimes are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:alpha-regimes}.

\paragraph{Fiducial state learning rate and exploitability threshold.}
The outer optimization loop over the fiducial state $\ket{\psi_0}$ is sensitive
to the exploitability threshold $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}}$ used in the inner Nash solver.
At $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} = 10^{-4}$, the solver may converge to different Nash equilibria
on the same orbit at successive optimization steps, producing sudden welfare drops (see Section~\ref{sec:welfare-drops}).
Tightening to $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} = 10^{-10}$ eliminates these discontinuities
but reduces the success rate of the inner solver, as some runs fail to converge entirely.
The combination of tighter thresholds with the adaptive learning rate scheduling resolves this trade-off.

\paragraph{Multiple Nash equilibria per orbit.}
When the solver fails, an alternative strategy is to perturb the state within the same orbit
(by applying products of random local unitaries) and re-run the algorithm.
This keeps optimization running but reveals that multiple Nash equilibria can coexist on the same orbit,
manifested by jumping payoffs across restarts.
For the 6-player case, the landscape is particularly complex
(Figure~\ref{fig:six-player-solver}), with
the solver requiring up to 20 random restarts to find a valid equilibrium.

\section{Numerical results: diagonal payoff Hamiltonians}
\label{sec:results-diagonal}

\subsection{Three-player quantum Prisoner's Dilemma}

For the 3-player game with the payoff structure in Table~\ref{tab:payoffs},
the welfare-optimal Nash equilibrium is found on the GHZ orbit,
$\ket{\psi_0} \sim (\ket{000} + \ket{111})/\sqrt{2}$.
The optimization of the fiducial state consistently converges to this orbit
across multiple random initializations.

Not all runs succeed: Figure~\ref{fig:unsuccessful-3player} shows a case where
the algorithm converges to a locally stable state (at iteration 8401)
that has low exploitability by the local criterion but remains globally exploitable.

\subsection{Six-player results and bond dimension scaling}
\label{sec:six-player}

For $L = 6$ players with the ring-geometry pairwise Hamiltonian,
we swept over bond dimensions $\chi \in \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9\}$
with randomly initialized MPS states.
Counter-intuitively, higher bond dimensions do not uniformly improve outcomes:
the best welfare is observed at low $\chi$, while larger $\chi$ runs
exhibit more frequent welfare drops due to the richer Nash landscape

\subsection{Welfare drops and the topology of the Nash variety}
\label{sec:welfare-drops}

A persistent phenomenon in the fiducial state optimization is the occurrence of sudden welfare drops.
These drops are robust against:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Changing the gradient calculation to select the maximum-welfare perturbation.
    \item Adding ridge regularization to the gradient estimator.
\end{enumerate}

The welfare drops arise because the manifold of Nash equilibria in the pure state space
does not have consistent topology across different regions (Figure~\ref{fig:welfare-drops}).
At an $\varepsilon$-approximate Nash equilibrium with $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$,
a small perturbation of the fiducial state can cause the inner solver to converge
to a qualitatively different Nash equilibrium with drastically different payoffs.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{welfare_drop_case_study1}
        \caption{Welfare trajectory showing sudden drops.}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\textwidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{welfare_drop_case_study2}
        \caption{Corresponding payoff trajectories.}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Case study of sudden welfare drops during fiducial state optimization.
    The drops are robust against changes to the gradient estimator and regularization.}
    \label{fig:welfare-drops}
\end{figure}

The entanglement parameters (bipartite entropies), by contrast, change smoothly across these drops
(Figure~\ref{fig:entanglement-smooth}).
This is expected: the orbit is updated continuously, so the entanglement structure varies smoothly
even when the Nash equilibrium found on that orbit changes discontinuously.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{entanglement_params_smooth}
    \caption{Entanglement parameters evolve smoothly during optimization,
    even across welfare drops, confirming that the orbit changes continuously.}
    \label{fig:entanglement-smooth}
\end{figure}

Gradient diagnostic analysis reveals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The gradient norm increases sharply at larger perturbation strengths $\epsilon$,
    indicating that large gradients are artifacts of basin-jumping between Nash equilibria.
    \item The success rate of the inner solver drops significantly at larger $\epsilon$.
    \item The normalized gradient (welfare change divided by $\epsilon$) varies wildly,
    confirming that the gradient estimate becomes unreliable when perturbations cross basin boundaries.
\end{itemize}
These diagnostics are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-diagnostics}.
The resolution is to reduce the perturbation strength $\epsilon$ such that
$2\epsilon$ is smaller than the ``bottleneck'' of the connected component in the Nash variety,
ensuring that the optimization remains on a single continuous branch.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{gradient_diagnostics}
    \caption{Gradient diagnostics for the welfare drop analysis.
    Top left: gradient norm vs.\ perturbation strength.
    Top right: solver success rate.
    Bottom left: mean welfare change.
    Bottom right: normalized gradient.
    The sharp increase in gradient norm and drop in success rate at larger $\epsilon$
    confirm basin-jumping as the cause of welfare drops.}
    \label{fig:gradient-diagnostics}
\end{figure}

\section{Area laws and entanglement structure}
\label{sec:area-laws}

A quantum game is defined by the triple $(\hat{h}_i, G_i, N)$ where $N$ is the number of players,
$\hat{h}_i$ are the payoff Hamiltonians, and $G_i$ are the strategy groups.
We study the quantum advantage associated with the entanglement structure
of the underlying resource state.

\begin{itemize}
    \item In the product state orbit ($\chi = 1$), the game is purely classical,
    with automatic extension to mixed strategies by controlling the probability amplitudes of individual qubits.
    \item In the volume-law regime, local unitary invariance implies
    $\tr(U_i \rho U_i^\dagger \hat{O}) \approx \tr(\rho \hat{O})$
    for any local observable $\hat{O}$, so players have negligible strategic influence.
    \item There may therefore exist an optimal entanglement structure
    for the fiducial state that maximizes the quantum advantage
    in the sense of mechanism design---neither too little nor too much entanglement.
\end{itemize}

Our numerical results support this picture:
the welfare-optimal Nash equilibria for the quantum Prisoner's Dilemma
consistently reside on the GHZ orbit ($\chi = 2$),
and higher bond dimensions do not improve the optimal welfare.
This suggests that the best Nash equilibrium satisfies an area law for entanglement,
with the optimal resource state having $O(1)$ entanglement entropy across any bipartition.

\section{Non-commutative payoff Hamiltonians}
\label{sec:non-commutative}

\subsection{Introducing non-commutativity}

To move beyond the diagonal (classical) payoff structure, we consider Hamiltonians
with non-commuting terms. The key observations, organized by the degree of non-commutativity, are:

\paragraph{Product state regime.}
For product states, Nash equilibria are always found irrespective of the payoff Hamiltonians.
The algorithm converges reliably in this regime.

\paragraph{Ising-type perturbations.}
Adding entanglement via a multi-qubit unitary, one can find Nash equilibria
for random Ising games with
\begin{equation}
    \hat{h}_L = \hat{h}_0 + V(g_L), \quad \hat{h}_R = \hat{h}_0 + V(g_R),
\end{equation}
where $V(g) = g\, \hat{X}_i$ and $g_L, g_R$ are drawn from a random distribution.

\paragraph{Heisenberg-type interactions.}
The existence of Nash equilibria breaks down for random Heisenberg interactions
\begin{equation}
    \hat{h}_i = n_x \hat{X}_i \hat{X}_{i+1} + n_y \hat{Y}_i \hat{Y}_{i+1} + n_z \hat{Z}_i \hat{Z}_{i+1},
\end{equation}
where $(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ is a random unit vector on $S^2$.
Adding entanglement in the initial states causes the best response dynamics to end up in limit cycles,
preventing convergence to a Nash equilibrium.

\paragraph{Remark.}
All games considered so far are translationally invariant, i.e.,
$T^\dagger \hat{h}_i T = \hat{h}_i$ where $T$ is the translation operator.

\subsection{Three-player results with non-commutative perturbations}

To introduce non-commutativity gradually, we let $\hat{h}' = \hat{h} + V$
where $V$ is a random Hermitian matrix with bounded Frobenius norm.
For $\|V\|_F = 1$, the fiducial state optimization still converges to the GHZ orbit
(Figure~\ref{fig:noncomm-ghz}),
suggesting robustness of the GHZ orbit as the welfare-optimal state
even in the presence of non-commuting perturbations.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{noncomm_ghz_robustness}
    \caption{Fiducial state optimization with non-commutative perturbations
    ($\|V\|_F = 1$). Compared to the standard (diagonal) QPD runs,
    the optimization still converges to the GHZ orbit,
    suggesting robustness of maximal entanglement as the optimal resource state.}
    \label{fig:noncomm-ghz}
\end{figure}

The relationship between entanglement parameters and equilibrium welfare
remains qualitatively the same across different non-commutativity strengths
(Figure~\ref{fig:noncomm-entanglement}).
The success rate of the inner Nash solver drops to approximately 60\%,
but the optimal orbit remains the GHZ orbit when equilibria are found.

The \texttt{compute\_exploitability()} function was modified to handle the expanded strategy space
arising from non-commuting Hamiltonians,
where the single-player best response is no longer one-dimensional.

\subsection{Two-player non-commutative games}

For 2-player games, we map out the exploitability landscape
as a function of the entanglement in the fiducial state (Figure~\ref{fig:twoplayer}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Local solvers fail to find Nash equilibria once the state
    has nonzero Schmidt eigenvalues (low-lying entangled orbits).
    \item Convergence to Nash equilibria is successful in the product state regime.
    \item Using damped-step best response dynamics destabilizes the optimization,
    while full-step best response gives correct results.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{twoplayer_product_state}
    \caption{Two-player non-commutative game with product states.
    The algorithm converges successfully in the product state regime,
    but fails for entangled states with nonzero Schmidt eigenvalues.}
    \label{fig:twoplayer}
\end{figure}

Explicit search over the full strategy space confirms that
the exploitability does not reach zero for entangled states
with non-commutative payoffs,
with the minimal exploitability consistent with the local algorithm at approximately $0.01$.
This suggests a genuine obstruction to Nash equilibria in the entangled regime
for non-commuting Hamiltonians, rather than a failure of the algorithm.

\subsection{Open questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sequential (instead of simultaneous) updates for the best response solver
    may improve convergence for non-commutative payoffs.
    \item A differential best response solver without SVD decomposition
    could avoid the instabilities observed with damped steps.
    \item Double-oracle methods for expanding subgames may provide
    a path to equilibria in cases where the current algorithm fails.
    \item CMA-ES navigation on the exploitability landscape
    could find minima that local gradient methods miss,
    though the computational cost (requiring differential evolution subroutine calls
    for each update) may be prohibitive.
    \item Testing with completely random payoff Hamiltonians:
    does the optimal orbit remain the maximally entangled one?
\end{enumerate}
